The biggest El Niño in recorded history is forming right now.

NOAA puts the odds at 69% that this becomes the strongest El Niño ever measured — and it peaks in three months. Hundreds of millions of people will feel it in their power, their water, their livelihoods. El Niño Climate Week points the world's largest climate gathering at the world's largest climate event.

No El Niño has ever been this warm, this early.

The 2026 El Niño is shaping up to be the strongest in modern records, and it's building on a baseline that gets warmer every cycle. The forecast is not the hard part. The hard part is what happens between the forecast and the flood.

Loss figures are cumulative global economic losses attributed to each event over the onset year and the three years after — about 4–5% of global GDP at the time (Liu et al., Nature Communications, 2023, figures as published). Even on NOAA’s new Relative ONI, which subtracts the background warming of the whole tropical ocean, 2026 is tracking the strongest events ever recorded — and the absolute ocean heat is unprecedented either way: 29.0 °C in Niño 3.4, the warmest May–July ever measured.

If this is “just another 2015” — remember what 2015 actually did.

  • 60–100 million people pushed into food insecurity worldwide
  • ~100,000 premature deaths estimated from Indonesia’s drought-fueled fire haze
  • 10.2 million people needing food aid in Ethiopia’s worst drought in decades
  • The longest global coral bleaching event ever recorded
  • 2016 became the hottest year in recorded history
  • $3.9 trillion in global economic losses

1997–98 — one notch weaker — killed roughly 23,000 people in floods, storms, and droughts. Forecast models now put 2026’s peak above both.

Power

Heat waves, storms, and drought-strained hydropower push grids past their limits — exactly when people need cooling, pumping, and communication most.

💧

Water

El Niño redraws the rain map: drought where communities expect rain, floods where they don't. Reservoirs, wells, and crops all sit downstream of that shift.

🏠

Livelihoods

Fisheries collapse, harvests fail, supply chains snarl. The people hit hardest are the ones who never attend a climate conference.

New York Climate Week shouldn't look like a party to the other 8 billion people.

Every September, the climate community gathers in New York — breakfasts, run clubs, networking dinners. To the people who will actually live through this El Niño, that can look like climate isn't all that urgent.

Imagine instead that the week pointed at the storm on the horizon: what the science says, what's already happening, and what we can still do about power systems, water systems, and communication before the peak. A climate conversation that isn't just for climate people — one led by everyone on Earth.

Imagine we come out the other side of El Niño with something different to say about the climate — because of what we did together.

"Nothing we can do" is wrong. Here's the work.

Between Climate Week and the El Niño peak there are roughly three months. That's not long enough to stop it — it's exactly long enough to prepare for it, and to prove that a climate event can be met with a plan instead of a shrug.

Power systems

Harden grids for heat and storms, stage backup power for critical services, and get demand-response plans in place before the stress arrives.

Water systems

Pre-position storage and treatment where drought and flood risk is elevated, and get reservoir and well contingency plans out of the drawer.

Communication

Forecasts only matter if they reach people in time, in their language, with something actionable attached. Build the channels now.

The conversation

Use a live, global climate event to expand who talks about climate — from the people already at the conference to the people living the impacts.
